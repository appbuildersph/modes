# Kalma: Evidence for Calm, Low-Stimulus Digital Math Practice

## Math difficulties hardest for children with ADHD

Working memory deficits are the primary neurocognitive mechanism linking ADHD to math difficulty. Meta-analyses show large effect sizes (d=0.55 to d=2.15) for working memory impairment in ADHD. Math achievement scores show an effect size of d=0.67 below peers without ADHD. Children with ADHD show reduced math fluency despite similar calculation scores, plus higher error rates due to inattention and difficulty tracking multi-step procedures.

**Sources (peer-reviewed):**
- Alloway, T. P., & Alloway, R. G. (2010). Investigating working memory and deficits in ADHD. Journal of Attention Disorders, 13(4), 440-456. DOI: 10.1177/1087054709347277

---

## Math difficulties hardest for autistic children

Autism spectrum disorder shows an effect size of g=-0.696 in math scores versus typically developing peers, with higher variability. Approximately 25% of autistic learners have co-occurring dyscalculia. Specific challenges include executive function deficits affecting multi-step word problems and sequential operation tracking.

**Sources (peer-reviewed):**
- Tonizzi, I., & Usai, M. C. (2023). Math abilities in autism spectrum disorder: A meta-analysis. Research in Developmental Disabilities, 139, 104559. DOI: 10.1016/j.ridd.2023.104559
- Karal, M. A., & Riccomini, P. J. (2024). Meta-analysis of mathematics interventions for learners with autism spectrum disorder. Education and Training in Autism and Developmental Disabilities, 59(3), 269-281. DOI: 10.1177/215416472405900303

---

## Co-occurring math learning difficulties

28-78% of autistic children meet criteria for ADHD (high heterogeneity). 15-25% of children with ADHD have dyscalculia. 22.7% of children with one neurodevelopmental disorder have a second. Dyscalculia and dyslexia co-occur in 40-70% of cases.

---

## Visual environment, decorative elements, and attention

Classroom visual environment directly affects learning. In a randomized controlled study of kindergarten children, those in a highly decorated classroom were off-task 39% of the time versus 28% in a sparse classroom; decorated classroom learners gained 18% on test scores versus 33% in sparse. The seductive details effect (adding interesting but irrelevant information) reduces learning; a meta-analysis of 39 studies found medium effects for retention and transfer.

**Sources (peer-reviewed):**
- Fisher, A. V., Godwin, K. E., & Seltman, H. (2014). Visual environment, attention allocation, and learning in young children: When too much of a good thing may be bad. Psychological Science, 25(7), 1362-1370. DOI: 10.1177/0956797614533801
- Rey, G. D. (2012). A review of research and a meta-analysis of the seductive detail effect. Educational Research Review, 7(3), 216-237. DOI: 10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003

---

## Predictability and routine for autistic learners

Errorless learning (instruction preventing errors) is evidence-based for autism (NCAEP 2020: 28 identified practices). Structured teaching with predictable routines reduces anxiety and supports learning. Visual supports are a distinct NCAEP-identified practice.

**Sources (peer-reviewed / official):**
- Wong, C., Odom, S. L., Hume, K. A., Cox, A. W., Fettig, A., Kucharczyk, S., ... & Schultz, T. R. (2015). Evidence-based practices for children, youth, and young adults with autism spectrum disorder: A comprehensive review. Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 45(7), 1951-1966. DOI: 10.1007/s10803-014-2351-z

## ADHD factors: session length, breaks, chunking

Younger children with ADHD benefit from work chunks of 2-8 minutes; older children do better with 8-15 minute chunks. Breaking tasks into small pieces reduces cognitive load. No peer-reviewed studies identify optimal session length for ADHD math practice; "5 questions per session" is inference based on chunking principles, not empirically derived.

---

## Computer-assisted instruction for ADHD and autism

**ADHD:** Mautone et al. (2005) found that children's mathematics achievement improved and on-task behavior increased during CAI sessions relative to independent seatwork. Rabiner et al. (2010) conducted an RCT comparing Computerized Attention Training (CAT) and Computer Assisted Instruction (CAI) in 77 first graders with inattention; attention intervention effects were modest at follow-up, and effect sizes for math outcomes in later meta-analyses range from d=0.42-0.43 (between-subjects designs).

**Autism:** CAI enhances academic skills in students with ASD, but the evidence base is less rigorous than for ADHD.

**Sources (peer-reviewed):**
- Mautone, J. A., DuPaul, G. J., & Jitendra, A. K. (2005). The effects of computer-assisted instruction on the mathematics performance and classroom behavior of children with ADHD. Journal of Attention Disorders, 9(1), 301-312. DOI: 10.1177/1087054705278832
- Rabiner, D. L., Murray, D. W., Skinner, A. T., & Malone, P. S. (2010). A randomized trial of two promising computer-based interventions for students with attention difficulties. Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology, 38(1), 131-142. DOI: 10.1007/s10802-009-9353-x

## Sensory sensitivity

Sensory over-responsivity affects 16.5% of elementary school children (7-11 years). Auditory sensitivity can lead to anxiety, overwhelm, and avoidance in classroom settings. WCAG 2.2 accessibility standards require controls for motion (SC 2.2.2: pause/stop for auto-play animations longer than 5 seconds) and prohibit flashing content (SC 2.3.1: no more than three flashes per second).

**Sources (peer-reviewed / official standards):**
- Ben-Sasson, A., Hen, L., Fluss, R., Cermak, S. A., Engel-Yeger, B., & Gal, E. (2009). A meta-analysis of sensory modulation symptoms in individuals with autism spectrum disorders. Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 39(1), 1-11. DOI: 10.1007/s10803-008-0593-3
- W3C Web Accessibility Initiative. (2023). Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2. Retrieved from www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/

## Kalma design rules: evidence status

| Kalma rule | Evidence status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Plain background (no decorations) | Supported | Fisher et al. 2014: decorated vs sparse classrooms; test gains 33% vs 18%, off-task 28% vs 39% |
| No music, coins, rewards, gamification | Mixed | Seductive details effect (Rey 2012) suggests decorative/irrelevant features impair learning; serious games show attention gains but may overload novices |
| One question per screen | Inference | Reduces visual clutter and working-memory load; aligns with Fisher et al. 2014 and cognitive load theory, but not directly tested |
| Same layout every time | Supported (autism) | Wong et al. 2015: predictability and structure are evidence-based; specific layout consistency untested |
| No "Correct!" flash or shake | Supported | Fisher et al. 2014 (visual clutter effects); WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.1 (no >3 flashes per second) |
| Calm tone, no negative feedback | Supported (autism) | NCAEP errorless learning principles reduce learned avoidance; Wong et al. 2015 |
| Replies: max 2 sentences, one idea | Inference | Aligns with working-memory load reduction but untested in tutor interaction |
| About 5 questions per session | Untested | Aligns with chunking (2-15 min blocks) but specific count not empirically derived |

## Competency readiness for Kalma (inference)

**Tackle first:** Simple, low executive load: Grade 4 place value, decimal notation, fraction identification; Grade 5 clock time, prime vs composite; Grade 6 ratio description, percentage concepts. All at "madali" (easy) difficulty. Requires visual models on every question (fraction tiles, number lines, base-10 blocks).

**Tackle with scaffolding:** Single-operation procedures with visual supports (regrouping, multiplication facts, fraction comparison, decimal operations). Requires showing concrete image and visual model on screen for every question.

**Defer to Hakbang or hands-on:** Multi-step word problems, composite figures, tessellation, probability, 3D construction. High executive load or require exploratory learning unsuited to question-per-screen format.
