# Pakinig Mode: Evidence for Listening-First Math Practice

**Research scope:** Primary-age children (6-12) with dyslexia, low vision, or weak reading skills. Grade 4-6 MATATAG curriculum. Evidence from 2005-2026; Philippine context noted where available.

---

## How much do reading difficulties drive math difficulties?

### Takeaway

Reading comprehension and language skills predict word-problem performance far more strongly than calculation skills. Approximately 77% of children with dyslexia, ADHD, or dyscalculia have only one condition, indicating comorbidity is not universal. Language load in problem statements overwhelms working memory even when students can calculate correctly.

### Cited Findings

- Approximately 77% of children affected by dyslexia, ADHD, or dyscalculia experience only one condition; those with any learning disability are 2.1 to 3.1 times more likely to have a second condition. van Bergen, E., et al. (2025). "Co-Occurrence and Causality Among ADHD, Dyslexia, and Dyscalculia." *Psychological Science*, 36(3), 204-217. DOI: 10.1177/09567976241293999
- Early reading fluency in first grade predicts later math outcomes, particularly in word-problem solving; reading gaps tend to slow math growth in subsequent years.
- Reading comprehension and language skills predict word-problem performance more strongly than they predict basic calculation.
- Students with reading difficulties score lower on both word problems and mathematical vocabulary tasks compared to general students.
- Comorbidity between dyslexia and dyscalculia appears driven by correlated genetic risks rather than direct causality. Han, W. (2025). "Dyscalculia and dyslexia in school-aged children: comorbidity, support, and future prospects." *Frontiers in Education*, 10, 1515216. DOI: 10.3389/feduc.2025.1515216

### Inferences

- Removing language decoding load from word problems should improve word-problem performance more than calculation performance.
- Students with dyslexia but intact calculation skills will show large gains when language barriers are removed.
- Pakinig's listening-first approach targets the primary bottleneck: language comprehension, not numerical reasoning.

### Gaps

- No Philippine studies measuring dyslexia-dyscalculia overlap or word-problem language load in Filipino/Tagalog contexts.
- Exact prevalence of dyslexia-math disability overlap in primary-age populations remains unclear.

## How much do read-aloud and text-to-speech accommodations help math?

### Takeaway

Read-aloud accommodations improve math performance less than reading performance. Math effects are smaller but meaningful when targeting word-problem comprehension. Elementary students with learning disabilities benefit more from read-aloud than non-disabled peers; this pattern reverses in secondary school. Benefit depends on classroom integration; students unfamiliar with TTS during instruction may be hindered during testing.

### Cited Findings

- Buzick, H., & Stone, E. (2014). "A Meta-Analysis of Research on the Read Aloud Accommodation." *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice*, 33(3), 17-30. DOI: 10.1111/emip.12040. Found smaller effects for math than reading.
- Li, H. (2014). "The Effects of Read-Aloud Accommodations for Students With and Without Disabilities: A Meta-Analysis." *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice*, 33(3), 3-16. DOI: 10.1111/emip.12027. Meta-analysis of 114 effect sizes from 23 studies; human proctors more effective than digital delivery.
- Wood, S. G., Moxley, J. H., Tighe, E. L., & Wagner, R. K. (2018). "Does Use of Text-to-Speech and Related Read-Aloud Tools Improve Reading Comprehension for Students With Reading Disabilities? A Meta-Analysis." *Journal of Learning Disabilities*, 51(1), 73-84. DOI: 10.1177/0022219416688170. Weighted effect size d = 0.35 (95% CI: 0.14 to 0.56).
- Text-to-speech is most effective for students with visual impairments, reading struggles, dyscalculia, or visual processing difficulties when integrated into classroom instruction.
- Students not exposed to TTS regularly in the classroom may be hindered by its use during testing.

### Inferences

- Pakinig as classroom instruction (not one-off accommodation) should build familiarity and confidence through repeated exposure.
- AI tutor voice matching teacher speech patterns may approximate human-read-aloud effectiveness.
- Secondary students without disabilities may not need Pakinig; primary users are elementary students with reading difficulties.

### Gaps

- No RCTs comparing persistent TTS instruction versus one-off accommodation use in math.
- Limited evidence on effect size for listening-first math over a full school year.

## What is the evidence for synchronized word highlighting during read-aloud?

### Takeaway

Synchronized word highlighting during read-aloud improves comprehension and reduces cognitive load. Bimodal reading (simultaneous visual and auditory input) improves word recall and reading ease especially for children with dyslexia. The combination of visual tracking cues with auditory input produces measurable gains.

### Cited Findings

- Comprehension increases and decoding effort decreases when readers track synchronized highlighting while listening to correct pronunciation.
- Bimodal learning (combined reading and listening) improves word recognition, vocabulary, comprehension, fluency, accuracy, and recall.
- Eye movement response is affected by highlighting; visual tracking cues interact with auditory input.
- Concurrent presentation of words in both auditory and visual channels produces significant gains in word recall.
- Replay capability extends benefit beyond synchronized playback sessions; downloadable audio versions support repetition.

### Inferences

- Pakinig's synchronized highlighting design is evidence-backed for dyslexia and low-vision readers.
- Highlighting color and size matter; design should optimize for accessibility with high contrast and sufficient letter size.
- Replay capability enhances learning; users should repeat questions as needed.

### Gaps

- Limited research on optimal highlighting color, duration, and positioning for primary-age children.
- No studies on interaction between highlighting and speed control (0.8x, 0.65x, 1x).

## What does the evidence say on typography and layout?

### Takeaway

Increased letter and word spacing improves reading speed significantly for children with dyslexia. Line length should be 40-60 characters for primary-age children; longer lines reduce comprehension. Specialized dyslexia fonts (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie) do not outperform standard fonts with proper spacing and contrast. Large print is 18pt or larger; contrast ratios must meet WCAG AA (4.5:1) or AAA (7:1).

### Cited Findings

**Letter and Word Spacing**

- Zorzi, M., Barbiero, C., Facoetti, A., et al. (2012). "Extra-large letter spacing improves reading in dyslexia." *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 109(28), 11455-11459. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1205566109. Substantially improved reading performance in Italian and French dyslexic children.
- WCAG 1.4.12 user-override requirement specifies: letter spacing at least 0.12em of font size; word spacing at least 0.16em; line height at least 1.5x font size. (WCAG specifies user override capability, not evidence of optimal spacing values.)
- When increasing letter spacing, word spacing must grow proportionally (at least 3x letter spacing) so words remain distinct.

**Specialized Dyslexia Fonts**

- Wery, J. J., & Diliberto, J. A. (2017). "The effect of a specialized dyslexia font, OpenDyslexic, on reading rate and accuracy." *Annals of Dyslexia*, 67(2), 114-127. DOI: 10.1007/s11881-016-0127-1. Open-access.
- Kuster, S. M., van Weerdenburg, M., Gompel, M., & Bosman, A. M. T. (2018). "Dyslexie font does not benefit reading in children with or without dyslexia." *Annals of Dyslexia*, 68(1), 25-42. DOI: 10.1007/s11881-017-0154-6. Open-access.
- Atkinson Hyperlegible was designed for low-vision readers with distinguishable letterforms. No peer-reviewed evidence demonstrates dyslexia-specific advantage over standard fonts with proper spacing and contrast.

**Font Size and Contrast**

- WCAG 1.4.3 (Contrast Minimum, AA): Larger fonts (18+ pt or 14 pt bold) require only 3:1 contrast ratio; typical fonts (12 pt or smaller) require 4.5:1.
- WCAG 1.4.6 (Contrast Enhanced, AAA): 7:1 contrast for normal text.
- Studies of second and fifth graders show: decreasing font size while increasing line length significantly lowered comprehension scores in younger readers.

**Line Length**

- Optimal line length for children: 40-60 characters. Young readers with weak reading skills perform better with approximately seven words per line (roughly 40-50 characters).
- Subjects reading narrow paragraphs had better retention than those reading wide paragraphs.

**Low-Vision Reading**

- Standard print: 10-12 pt; large print: 18 pt or larger.
- Students' vision varies greatly; individualize magnification based on eye condition, age, learning style, and additional challenges.

### Inferences

- Pakinig's black question panel with wide letter and line spacing aligns with evidence from WCAG 1.4.12 and extra-large spacing studies.
- Question line length should stay under 60 characters (ideally 40-50 for younger primary students) to support weak readers.
- Standard fonts with proper spacing and contrast are as evidence-supported as specialized dyslexia fonts.
- Atkinson Hyperlegible defensible for low-vision users but not uniquely beneficial for dyslexia.

### Gaps

- No studies on interaction between letter spacing and font size for primary-age children.
- Limited evidence on highlighting color effects on comprehension in high-contrast scenarios.
- No Philippine studies on optimal typography for children reading English versus Filipino versus Taglish.

## How should spoken math work?

### Takeaway

Natural phrasing math speech (analogous to classroom teacher language) is more intuitive than maximum-precision notation speech. Fractions, equations, and symbols require careful verbalization: "three fourths" not "3/4"; "equals" not "="; "times" for multiplication. Graphs, coordinate systems, protractors, symmetry, and angles are inaccessible without vision and require alternatives: audio description, tactile graphics, verbal scaffolding, or item skip. MathML markup enables screen reader navigation of complex equations.

### Cited Findings

**Spoken Math Conventions**

- ClearSpeak (developed with U.S. Department of Education support) speaks mathematical expressions the way teachers speak in classrooms, with unambiguous adjustments for notation, fractions, and symbols. (Vendor documentation: WIRIS MathType accessibility guide.)
- No peer-reviewed studies with primary-age children compare different math-speech conventions.
- Graphical representations (charts, graphs, coordinate systems) are inaccessible without visual description or tactile alternatives.
- Equations embedded as images are completely inaccessible; accessible solutions include LaTeX/MathML markup, MathJax for web rendering, and tactile graphics.

### Inferences

- Pakinig tutor rule "replies written for listening, with no symbols or markdown" aligns with natural-language math principles.
- Avoid requiring voice input of fractions or multi-digit numbers due to speech recognition constraints.
- Design rule "skip chip when picture-only question skipped" is evidence-backed for inaccessible items (angles, symmetry, graphs, protractors).

### Gaps

- No studies on spoken math conventions with primary-age children.
- Limited guidance on verbalizing measurement units, decimals, ratios, and percentages for primary students.
- No evidence on whether repeated exposure to natural-language math improves students' mathematical language development.

## What is known about voice answers for math?

### Takeaway

Speech-to-text is effective for writing (longer output, fewer spelling errors) across disability categories but shows mixed results for reading comprehension. Children's speech recognition accuracy is far lower than adults'. Isolated numbers and fractions are particularly difficult to recognize, making voice input of numeric answers unreliable. Speech-to-text requires explicit classroom instruction to be effective.

### Cited Findings

**Speech Input for Writing**

- Writing output is significantly longer with fewer mechanics errors when speech-to-text is used across learning disabilities, emotional disturbance, and traumatic brain injury.
- Students demonstrate positive perceptions of speech-to-text and report greater independence.
- Reading comprehension gains are inconsistent; most effective when paired with explicit instruction and teacher guidance.

**Children's Speech Recognition Accuracy**

- Recent research on children's automatic speech recognition (ASR) shows ongoing challenges:
  - Graave, T., Li, Z., Lohrenz, T., & Fingscheidt, T. (2024). "Mixed Children/Adult/Childrenized Fine-Tuning for Children's ASR." *Interspeech 2024*, 5188-5192. DOI: 10.21437/interspeech.2024-499
  - Zhang, Y., Yue, Z., Patel, T., & Scharenborg, O. (2024). "Improving child speech recognition with augmented child-like speech." *Interspeech 2024*, 5183-5187. DOI: 10.21437/interspeech.2024-485
  - Gale, R., Chen, L., Dolata, J., van Santen, J., & Asgari, M. (2019). "Improving ASR Systems for Children with Autism and Language Impairment Using Domain-Focused DNN Transfer Techniques." *Interspeech 2019*, 11-15. DOI: 10.21437/interspeech.2019-3161
- Literature indicates children's speech recognition error rates substantially exceed adult baselines; isolated number recognition is particularly challenging compared to continuous speech.
- Fine-tuning on diverse datasets can reduce child speech error rates significantly in emerging research.

**Filipino/Tagalog Speech Recognition**

- Recent research shows progress on Filipino/Tagalog ASR systems for children, but accurate systems for Filipino-speaking children remain limited.
- Age-related variations in pronunciation, pacing, and intonation create ongoing challenges.

### Inferences

- Pakinig design rule "large mic button for voice answers; typing still works" is appropriate given voice accuracy constraints.
- Typing should remain default or preferred; voice input should be optional and clearly not required for success.
- Avoid requiring voice input of isolated numbers, fractions, or multi-word numeric phrases.
- Speech-to-text for open-ended explanation (justification, reasoning) may help learners with weak writing skills; numeric transcription is unreliable.

### Gaps

- No peer-reviewed studies on speech recognition accuracy for mathematical speech (numbers, fractions, symbols, equations) in children's voices.
- Limited research on code-switching (Taglish) speech recognition accuracy for Philippine children.
- Sparse evidence on whether explicit phonetic instruction improves children's speech clarity for ASR systems.

## Philippine context: Language and learning

### Takeaway

Reading difficulties in the Philippines are significant; many elementary learners struggle with comprehension in English and Filipino. Code-switching (Taglish) supports comprehension and helps students express ideas more fluidly. Language mismatch between home and school acts as a barrier to learning; students perform better when school language matches home language. Dyslexia is largely undiagnosed in the Philippines; limited prevalence data and low teacher awareness exist.

### Cited Findings

- Philippine Informal Reading Inventory (Phil-IRI) data show many Grade 4-6 students struggle with word recognition and comprehension.
- Phonological awareness and naming speed are significant predictors for reading in both Filipino and English.
- Code-switching (Taglish) is effective for developing comprehension; teachers recognize it as a pedagogical resource.
- Language mismatch acts as a barrier to learning; students perform better when school language matches home language.
- Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE) policy shows promise but faces inconsistent implementation.
- 2018 PISA: Philippines ranked lowest in reading comprehension among 79 countries; 80% of students below minimum proficiency.
- Dyslexia prevalence in Philippines: no 10-year dataset exists. Only 648 schools offered inclusive education as of 2019. Dyslexia remains underdiagnosed in most public schools.

### Inferences

- Gabay's three-language support (English, Tagalog, Taglish) aligns with evidence that code-switching supports comprehension.
- Pakinig may be particularly valuable for students whose home language is Filipino, Ilocano, Bisaya, etc.; listening can bypass English decoding difficulties.
- No need to screen for dyslexia before offering Pakinig; low diagnosis rates mean most affected students are not formally identified.

### Gaps

- No Phil-IRI data broken down by dyslexia status or specific reading profile.
- No studies on code-switching effects specific to math word problems.
- No data on how many Grade 4-6 students benefit from listening-first versus text-first instruction.
