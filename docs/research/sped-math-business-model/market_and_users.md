# Philippine Market for Blind and Low-Vision Learners (Grades 4-6)

## Market Size: Enrolled and Out-of-School Learners with Disabilities

### Takeaway
An estimated 5.1 million Filipino children have disabilities, but only 391,089 (8%) were enrolled in public schools in SY 2024-2025, leaving 4.7 million out of school. Visual impairment is a significant component: 57% of individuals with severe disability experience vision problems, but specific child numbers are unavailable.

### Cited Findings
- Estimated 5.1 million children with disabilities nationwide — [EDCOM 2 Study, June 2026](https://thepost.net.ph/news/nation/edcom-2-study-warns-5m-filipino-children-with-disabilities-still-underserve/)
- Only 391,089 learners with disabilities (LWDs) enrolled in DepEd public schools, SY 2024-2025 (8% enrollment rate) — [DepEd/IDinsight policy brief via Inquirer](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/1967506/deped-77-of-learners-so-far-enrolled-for-school-year-2024-2025/)
- Among enrolled LWDs, 61% categorized only by observable manifestations without formal medical diagnosis — [DepEd LIS data, 2025](https://thepost.net.ph/news/nation/edcom-2-study-warns-5m-filipino-children-with-disabilities-still-underserve/)
- Visual impairment is a major disability: 57% of individuals aged 15+ with severe disability have vision problems (2016 data) — [Philippine Statistics Authority, National Disability Prevalence Survey 2016](https://psa.gov.ph/statistics/national-disability-prevalence-survey)
- Among those with severe disability (2016), seeing was the second most affected function in daily life at 23%, after mobility at 41% — [PSA NDPS 2016](https://psa.gov.ph/statistics/national-disability-prevalence-survey)

### Inferences
- If 57% of the 5.1M children with disabilities have vision-related issues, roughly 2.9 million children may have visual impairment (blind, low vision, refractive errors). Grade 4-6 age cohort (~3 years of a ~13-year disability span) is approximately 23% of this, suggesting ~670k visually impaired children in target grades.
- Out-of-school children with visual impairment likely number in the hundreds of thousands, representing significant unmet demand.
- Early identification challenge: 61% of enrolled LWDs lack formal diagnosis, suggesting many visually impaired children are either misclassified or never identified.

### Gaps
- Philippines does not publish disability-type breakdowns (visual impairment vs. hearing vs. physical) for enrolled LWDs; EDCOM 2 study refers to aggregated "LWD" numbers only.
- PSA National Disability Prevalence Survey 2016 covered only adults 15+; no pediatric visual impairment prevalence data found for children under 15.
- 2020 Census data on disability exists but age-specific visual impairment breakdown not found in public releases.
- No data on what percentage of out-of-school children with disabilities have visual impairment specifically.

## Current Infrastructure: Special Schools, Centers, and Organizations

### Takeaway
The Philippines has only one national school for the blind (PNSB), and an estimated 60% of learners with disabilities have zero access to any SPED facility. Organizations like Resources for the Blind Inc. (RBI) provide training and braille services but reach only a small fraction of the population.

### Cited Findings
- Philippine National School for the Blind (PNSB) is the only school in the nation offering upper-level education to visually impaired students; 144 students enrolled in 2017 (most recent data found) — [Various sources including RBI and FTCJ](https://ftcj.org/?p=5294/)
- 60% of learners with disabilities have no access to any Special Needs Education (SPED) centers, Inclusive Learning Resource Centers (ILRCs), or SNED teachers — [DepEd/IDinsight, EDCOM 2, 2026](https://thepost.net.ph/news/nation/edcom-2-study-warns-5m-filipino-children-with-disabilities-still-underserve/)
- Resources for the Blind Inc. (RBI), founded 1988, operates three offices (Manila, Davao, Cebu) with 50 blindness specialists; produces ~5,000 braille textbook volumes annually and runs Computer Eyes training program — [ABBYY/RBI case study](https://www.abbyy.com/en-us/casestudies/abbyy-finereader-helps-resources-for-the-blind-to-produce-braille-textbooks/)
- RBI equipped with braille embossers, scanners, screenreader software, and computer training centers — [ABBYY case study, Vera Files](https://verafiles.org/?p=22892/)
- PNSB received braille embosser and JAWS screen reader in August 2017; most institutional computers use free NVDA software — [NFB article via Multiple sources](https://nfb.org/node/6514/)

### Inferences
- PNSB's 144 students (even if current data is higher) can serve only ~0.2% of estimated visually impaired school-age children, creating massive unmet demand.
- RBI's braille production (5,000 volumes/year) and three-city footprint cannot serve 2.9M visually impaired children nationally; regional and rural learners especially underserved.
- 60% of LWDs with zero SPED access implies most blind/low-vision children outside major cities receive no specialized instruction.
- Itinerant teacher model mentioned as gap-filler, but teacher shortages (see below) limit its effectiveness.

### Gaps
- Current enrollment data for PNSB (last confirmed 2017); no 2024-2025 figures found.
- Specific enrollment/capacity of other blind schools or SPED centers serving visual impairment nationally.
- Exact cost of RBI services (braille production, training programs) and who funds them (government, donors, private).
- Number of Inclusive Learning Resource Centers (ILRCs) and their coverage by province.

## Teacher Supply and Training

### Takeaway
The Philippines faces a critical SPED teacher shortage: 7,651 teachers short of demand for 323,344 learners with disabilities, and fast turnover, especially to overseas work, compounds the deficit. Teachers trained specifically for visual impairment are a subset of this already-scarce pool.

### Cited Findings
- Only 5,147 SPED teachers nationwide for 323,344 learners with disabilities aged 2-17 (SY 2023-2024), representing a shortage of 7,651 teachers — [Philstar, December 2024](https://www.philstar.com/cebu-news/2024/12/09/2406126/over-7000-sned-teachers-needed/)
- Fast turnover among SPED teachers in National Capital Region, with teachers leaving after 6 months due to higher overseas salary opportunities — [Various education policy sources](https://www.philstar.com/cebu-news/2024/12/09/2406126/over-7000-sned-teachers-needed/)
- Itinerant Teacher/Resource Teacher model used to stretch limited SPED teacher supply across multiple schools — [DepEd programs](https://www.philstar.com/cebu-news/2024/12/09/2406126/over-7000-sned-teachers-needed/)
- SPED teachers are trained to work with children with hearing and visual difficulties — [Philippine education policy sources](https://www.philstar.com/cebu-news/2024/12/09/2406126/over-7000-sned-teachers-needed/)

### Inferences
- Ratio of SPED teachers to LWDs is 1:63, meaning each teacher would need to serve 63 learners on average (far exceeding sustainable caseloads).
- Visual impairment training is likely a small fraction of total SPED teacher training, so specialized VI teachers are even more scarce than average SPED figures suggest.
- Teacher shortage makes reliance on apps, tools, and self-paced learning critical; teacher-dependent models are not scalable.

### Gaps
- No data on number of teachers specifically trained for visual impairment vs. other disability types.
- No figures on supply of itinerant VI teachers by region.
- No longitudinal data on SPED teacher attrition or vacancy rates by year.
- No salary comparison data between Philippine SPED teachers and overseas alternatives.

## Device and Internet Access

### Takeaway
While 87% of Filipino adults own smartphones, cost is a significant burden for low-income households (averaging PHP 1,281/month for internet). Connectivity is severely uneven: Metro Manila 66%, urban 57%, but Muslim Mindanao only 4%. Approximately 47,000 DepEd schools lack internet, disproportionately affecting rural learners with disabilities.

### Cited Findings
- 87% of Filipino adults are mobile phone users; nearly all respondents own a smartphone (2018-2021 surveys) — [PIDS, Statista, media landscape sources](https://www.pids.gov.ph/details/the-great-divide/)
- 47% of Filipinos use the internet overall; internet use skewed toward urban (57%) and Metro Manila (66%) — [PIDS study, Philippine Development Institute](https://www.pids.gov.ph/details/the-great-divide/)
- Internet connectivity in Muslim Mindanao only 4% (most severe regional gap) — [PIDS analysis](https://www.pids.gov.ph/details/the-great-divide/)
- Average Filipino household spends PHP 1,281 per month on internet connection — [PIDS research](https://www.pids.gov.ph/details/the-great-divide/)
- Cost-effective free or zero-rated apps (YouTube, Facebook) dominate educational usage among Filipino students — [Various market research sources](https://www.imarcgroup.com/philippines-education-apps-market)
- Approximately 47,000 DepEd schools without internet access; government target to connect all schools by end of 2025 — [DepEd statements, Inquirer, 2024-2025](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/2080790/deped-to-connect-all-public-schools-to-the-internet-within-2025/)
- Bayanihan SIM program: 113,000+ learners and 357 public schools in geographically isolated and disadvantaged areas received free SIM cards with data — [DepEd/DICT program, 2024-2025](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/2080790/deped-to-connect-all-public-schools-to-the-internet-within-2025/)
- National Fiber Backbone Phase 2-3 launched to extend connectivity to remote areas; 772 free WiFi sites planned in Regions XI and XIII — [DICT/DepEd programs, 2024-2025](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/2080790/deped-to-connect-all-public-schools-to-the-internet-within-2025/)

### Inferences
- PHP 1,281/month internet cost is prohibitive for households below poverty line (PHP ~50,000/year or ~PHP 4,200/month); represents 25-30% of very low-income household income.
- Offline-capable apps (like Pakinig) are essential: rural and low-income learners with disabilities cannot rely on continuous connectivity.
- Free data programs (Bayanihan SIM) and government WiFi expansion create opportunity windows but coverage remains patchy through 2025.
- Smartphone ownership is high enough among Filipino households to be primary learning device if apps optimize for low-bandwidth or offline use.

### Gaps
- No specific data on device ownership among households with members with disabilities (PWD households).
- No breakdown of internet access by income quintile or disability status.
- Current number of schools actually connected under government programs not specified (only goals/plans mentioned).
- No data on sustainable offline vs. online app usage patterns for learners with disabilities.

## Economic Ability to Pay

### Takeaway
Most PWD households lack discretionary income for premium education apps. Government provides 20% discount on goods/services and educational support (scholarships, grants, subsidies for materials) under RA 10754, but these are not universal. Filipino education app market shows strong preference for free platforms; median willingness to pay for education services is very low.

### Cited Findings
- RA 10754 (2016) grants PWD 20% discount and VAT exemption on medical services, medicines, travel, hotels, and recreation — [Republic Act 10754, NCDA, multiple sources](https://jur.ph/law/summary/expanding-benefits-for-persons-with-disability/)
- RA 10754 provides access to educational support including scholarships, grants, financial aid, subsidies for books/learning materials/uniforms for PWD pursuing primary to tertiary education — [Republic Act 10754 text and summaries](https://batasnatin.com/explainers/ra-10754-explained-expanded-benefits-and-privileges-for-pwds/)
- PWD identification required to claim benefits (PWD ID issued by city/municipal mayor, barangay captain, or national council) — [NCDA/RA 10754](https://jur.ph/law/summary/expanding-benefits-for-persons-with-disability/)
- Philippines education apps market reached PHP 124 million (USD ~2.2M) in 2024, projected to grow 21.44% CAGR to PHP 712M by 2033; market dominated by free or freemium models (YouTube, Facebook, Brainly, Khan Academy, Edukasyon.ph) — [IMARC Group, Statista, multiple sources](https://www.imarcgroup.com/philippines-education-apps-market)
- Mean willingness to pay for education services among Batangas City households: PHP 2,731.08/month for college educational insurance plans, but this does not reflect willingness to pay for individual apps — [UPLB household survey](https://www.ukdr.uplb.edu.ph/etd-undergrad/11601)
- Affordability and low/zero cost are key adoption drivers for Filipino education apps — [Market research sources](https://www.imarcgroup.com/philippines-education-apps-market)

### Inferences
- RA 10754 educational support exists but is not universal (requires PWD ID registration and claim process), and does not guarantee funding for every app or material.
- For-profit pricing is unlikely viable for mass-market blind learner app; household income of families with disabled members generally below national average.
- PWD discount (20%) may apply to app pricing or assistive tech, but is time-bound to the purchase (not ongoing).
- Market precedent shows Filipino learners strongly prefer free apps; paid premium apps unlikely to gain traction without large institutional (government, NGO) subsidy.

### Gaps
- No income or poverty data specifically for households with a member with visual impairment.
- No data on how many PWD families have claimed educational benefits or how much was disbursed.
- No specific market research on willingness to pay for disability-specific apps (only general education apps).
- No comparison of cost to families: braille materials vs. digital tools vs. apps.

## Existing Tools and Services Used Today

### Takeaway
Existing tools for blind learners include braille (produced by RBI and PNSB), free open-source screen readers (NVDA), paid commercial screenreaders (JAWS ~PHP 48,000), and face-to-face instruction at PNSB, RBI centers, and itinerant SPED teachers. No widely-deployed offline Filipino accessible math apps for Grades 4-6 were found. Reliance is primarily on braille and face-to-face instruction, creating opportunity for digital tools.

### Cited Findings
- JAWS commercial screen reader costs approximately PHP 48,000; NVDA is free, open-source screenreader for Windows, available since 2006 — [Inquirer Technology, FTCJ, Neil Squire Institute sources](https://technology.inquirer.net/?p=32375/)
- NVDA supports 50+ languages with speech synthesis and can convert text to braille with attached braille display — [Neil Squire Institute, NVDA documentation](https://www.neilsquire.ca/nvda-screen-reader/)
- Braille embossers and JAWS purchased by PNSB (2017); NVDA standard in most Philippine institutional computer labs — [FTCJ, NFB sources](https://ftcj.org/news/5291/)
- RBI operates Computer Eyes training program (partnership with IBM, launched 2000) for computer and job skills training for visually impaired high school and college students — [ABBYY, FTCJ, RBI sources](https://www.abbyy.com/en-us/casestudies/abbyy-finereader-helps-resources-for-the-blind-to-produce-braille-textbooks/)
- RBI produces ~5,000 braille textbook volumes annually, distributed nationally to students — [ABBYY case study](https://www.abbyy.com/en-us/casestudies/abbyy-finereader-helps-resources-for-the-blind-to-produce-braille-textbooks/)
- National Library of the Philippines reported lacking Filipiana materials in braille and large print — [Vera Files, 2018](https://verafiles.org/articles/national-library-lacks-filipiniana-materials-in-braille-and-large-print/)
- Two in three DepEd teachers reported lacking training and tools to screen learners with disabilities — [EDCOM 2 Study, 2026](https://thepost.net.ph/news/nation/edcom-2-study-warns-5m-Filipino-children-with-disabilities-still-underserve/)

### Inferences
- Braille is the dominant tool; however, production is slow (RBI: 5,000 volumes/year for 2.9M visually impaired children) and centralized, limiting reach.
- Screen reader adoption follows a two-tier model: institutional settings use free NVDA, wealthy individuals or well-funded organizations may afford JAWS. No commercial alternatives mentioned for Philippine market.
- Digital tools are limited: Computer Eyes focuses on employment skills for older students, not math curriculum for Grades 4-6.
- Teacher shortage and training gaps mean many blind learners have access only to braille, not screen readers or digital instruction.
- No mention of Tagalog-language accessible math apps in search results; opportunity gap is significant.

### Gaps
- Specific cost and production timelines for RBI braille textbooks.
- Pricing for Computer Eyes training program.
- Enrollment in Computer Eyes; reach as a percentage of target population.
- Existence of other Filipino-language math or STEM education apps for blind learners (negative search result, but not exhaustive).
- Use of braille abacus or other traditional math tools; no quantitative data on prevalence or effectiveness.

## Business Model Considerations

**Market Size Opportunity:**
- Conservative estimate: ~670,000 visually impaired children Grades 4-6 nationally, with 92% out of school or under-resourced.
- Realistic addressable market (2-3 years): 50,000–200,000 learners in public schools + out-of-school programs, if combined approach (direct app + teacher/NGO partnerships).

**Revenue Model Fit:**
1. **Freemium/Free for learners + institutional licensing** (most aligned): Learners use free offline app; schools, PNSB, RBI, SPED centers license or bulk-deploy. RA 10754 educational support may fund school purchases.
2. **NGO/Donor funding** (most realistic near-term): Partner with organizations like RBI, PNSB, UNICEF Philippines, or international disability funders to fund distribution and training.
3. **Government procurement** (aspirational): If app meets DepEd curriculum, pitch as SPED resource under DepEd procurement; requires certification and local NGO endorsement.

**Key Success Factors:**
- Offline-first design (critical for 47,000 unconnected schools, rural learners, cost-burdened households).
- Tagalog-language support (fills gap noted in National Library report).
- Voice-only interface (accessibility for blind learners; NVDA/TalkBack compatibility optional).
- Teacher/NGO bundling (address 60% with zero SPED access; requires training materials, not just app).
- PNSB or RBI partnership (institutional credibility, pilot site, teacher referrals).

**Market Risks:**
- Fragmented demand (learners scattered across provinces, low density outside major cities).
- Teacher capacity constraint (only 1 SPED teacher per ~63 LWDs; app cannot replace instruction but must support it).
- Competing free tools and DIY solutions (braille + abacus, face-to-face SPED, YouTube tutorials).
- Affordability ceiling (PWD families low-income; must be free or heavily subsidized).
